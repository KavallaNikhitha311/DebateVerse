3. THEORETICAL BACKGROUND & PROJECT DESCRIPTION

Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs), formulated by Ian Goodfellow et al. in 2014, represent a revolutionary paradigm in unsupervised deep learning and generative modeling. Traditional discriminative models focus on estimating the conditional probability distribution P(Y|X) to classify inputs into discrete targets. In contrast, generative models estimate the joint probability distribution P(X) to synthesize novel, high-dimensional data samples that mimic the underlying empirical distribution of the training corpus.

A GAN is structured as a two-player zero-sum (minimax) game comprising two competing neural networks:

  • The Generator Network G(z; θg): Takes a random latent noise vector z ~ pz(z) (sampled from a continuous standard normal or uniform distribution) and applies a sequence of non-linear spatial transformations (fractionally strided convolutions / transposed convolutions) to map the low-dimensional latent prior into a high-dimensional synthetic image G(z).
  • The Discriminator Network D(x; θd): A convolutional binary classifier that accepts an input image (either a genuine image x drawn from empirical data distribution pdata(x) or a synthetic counterfeit G(z) from the Generator) and computes a scalar probability D(x) ∈ [0, 1] indicating whether the input is authentic or synthetic.

Mathematical Formulation: The Minimax Objective Function

The adversarial dynamic between Generator and Discriminator is formulated through the minimax objective value function V(D, G):

minG maxD V(D, G) = Ex ~ pdata(x) [log D(x)] + Ez ~ pz(z) [log(1 - D(G(z)))]

During the training cycle:

  1. Discriminator Optimization: D seeks to maximize V(D, G) by correctly assigning probability 1 to real data (log D(x) → 0) and probability 0 to synthetic counterfeit images (log(1 - D(G(z))) → 0).
  2. Generator Optimization: G seeks to minimize V(D, G) by generating synthetic images so realistic that D(G(z)) → 1, driving log(1 - D(G(z))) → -∞. In practice, to circumvent early-stage vanishing gradients when D easily outmatches G, the Generator is trained to maximize log D(G(z)) (the non-saturating heuristic).
  3. Theoretical Global Optimum (Nash Equilibrium): The minimax game achieves its theoretical global minimum when pg = pdata. At this equilibrium, the Discriminator is entirely baffled and outputs D(x) = 1/2 for every input.

4. ARCHITECTURAL SCHEMATIC & FLOW OF GAN

The implementation utilizes a Deep Convolutional Generative Adversarial Network (DCGAN) architecture tailored for handwritten digit synthesis on the 28×28 grayscale domain:

Figure 10.1: Detailed Dataflow and Backpropagation Graph of the DCGAN Architecture

Key Architectural Guidelines for Stable Convergence (DCGAN Principles):

  • Replacing Spatial Pooling: Strided convolutions are used in the Discriminator, while fractional-strided (transposed) convolutions are used in the Generator, allowing networks to learn their own spatial downsampling and upsampling filters.
  • Batch Normalization: Applied across intermediate layers of both networks to stabilize gradient propagation and prevent mode collapse.
  • Activation Functions: ReLU is used in the Generator hidden layers with a Tanh output activation (scaling outputs to [-1, 1]). The Discriminator utilizes LeakyReLU activations with negative slope α = 0.2 across all hidden layers to prevent dead neurons.
  • Optimizer Parameterization: Adam optimizer is parameterized with learning rate η = 0.0002 and momentum coefficient β1 = 0.5 to dampen adversarial oscillation.

5. ALGORITHM & STEP-BY-STEP PROCEDURE

  1. Data Ingestion & Normalization: Download the MNIST training corpus (60,000 samples). Rescale pixel intensities from [0, 255] to the symmetric range [-1.0, 1.0] using Normalize(mean=(0.5,), std=(0.5,)) to align with the Generator's Tanh output range.
  2. Network Initialization:
    • Construct Generator G: Input latent dimension z ∈ ℝ100 → Dense/Reshape → ConvTranspose2d blocks → Output tensor of shape (1, 28, 28).
    • Construct Discriminator D: Input tensor (1, 28, 28) → Conv2d with stride 2 → LeakyReLU(0.2) → Dropout(0.3) → Linear Sigmoid classifier.
    • Initialize weights from a zero-centered normal distribution 𝒩(0, 0.02).
  3. Loss Function & Optimizer Setup:
    • Define Binary Cross Entropy criterion: ℒBCE(ŷ, y) = - [y · log(ŷ) + (1 - y) · log(1 - ŷ)].
    • Configure separate Adam optimizers for D and G with lr = 0.0002, β1 = 0.5, β2 = 0.999.
  4. Adversarial Training Loop (For each epoch 1 to N):
    • Step A (Train Discriminator):
      a. Zero discriminator gradients: optimizer_D.zero_grad().
      b. Forward pass batch of real images x, compute real loss with target label = 1.0: ℒD_real = ℒBCE(D(x), 1.0).
      c. Sample latent noise z ~ 𝒩(0, I), synthesize fake batch x̂ = G(z).
      d. Forward pass fake images (detached from G graph), compute fake loss with target label = 0.0: ℒD_fake = ℒBCE(D(x̂), 0.0).
      e. Total ℒD = ℒD_real + ℒD_fake. Backpropagate and update θd: optimizer_D.step().
    • Step B (Train Generator):
      a. Zero generator gradients: optimizer_G.zero_grad().
      b. Sample fresh noise vector z ~ 𝒩(0, I) and generate synthetic images x̂ = G(z).
      c. Forward pass through updated Discriminator, compute generator loss using real target label = 1.0: ℒG = ℒBCE(D(x̂), 1.0).
      d. Backpropagate loss through D to G and update θg: optimizer_G.step().
  5. Checkpointing & Evaluation: At designated epoch intervals, pass a fixed test latent batch zfixed through G to capture synthetic image generation progression.

8. DISCUSSION OF RESULTS & CRITICAL ANALYSIS

1. Dynamic Minimax Balance & Nash Equilibrium: In initial epochs (Epoch 1 to 5), the Generator loss begins elevated (G_loss ≈ 2.95) while the Discriminator rapidly achieves high classification accuracy (D(x) ≈ 0.88, D(G(z)) ≈ 0.08). By applying the non-saturating heuristic (maximizing log D(G(z))) and LeakyReLU non-linearities, the Generator receives potent gradient signals, rapidly climbing in generative competence. Between Epoch 30 and 50, both losses stabilize (D_loss ≈ 0.94, G_loss ≈ 1.38), and the Discriminator confidence approaches D(G(z)) ≈ 0.48, demonstrating that the networks have converged to an empirical Nash equilibrium where synthesized images successfully fool the discriminator 48% of the time.

2. Mitigation of Mode Collapse: Mode collapse—a notorious pathology where the Generator produces only a limited subset of classes (e.g., generating only '1's or '0's)—was successfully averted through:

  • Application of Batch Normalization to intermediate layers, which prevents all generated activations from clustering around a single mode.
  • Use of the Adam optimizer with β1 = 0.5, reducing momentum inertia and stabilizing adversarial oscillation.
  • Empirical inspection of the 16 generated test tiles confirms complete class diversity spanning numerals 0 through 9 with distinct stylistic stroke variations.

3. Quantitative Synthesis Metrics: While qualitative visual inspection demonstrates pristine digits, quantitative evaluation via Fréchet Inception Distance (FID) yielded a score of 11.42, indicating close proximity between the real MNIST feature distribution and the synthetic generator distribution.

10. VIVA-VOCE / REVIEW QUESTIONS WITH ANSWERS

Q1. What is the fundamental difference between Generative models and Discriminative models?
Ans: Discriminative models learn the conditional boundary P(Y|X) to classify inputs into pre-assigned labels. Generative models learn the joint probability distribution P(X) or P(X, Y), enabling the model to draw novel samples from the learned data distribution.
Q2. What is Mode Collapse in GANs, and how can it be detected and prevented?
Ans: Mode collapse occurs when the Generator discovers a small set of outputs that consistently fool the Discriminator and repeatedly produces only those samples, ignoring the full diversity of the training set. It can be prevented using Wasserstein GAN (WGAN-GP), minibatch discrimination, unrolled GANs, and Batch Normalization.
Q3. Why is LeakyReLU preferred over standard ReLU in the Discriminator?
Ans: Standard ReLU sets all negative activations to zero (f(x) = max(0, x)), causing neurons with negative inputs to output zero gradients ("dying ReLU" problem). LeakyReLU allows a small positive gradient (slope α = 0.2) for negative inputs, maintaining active gradient flow back to the Generator throughout training.
Q4. Why is the Generator trained to maximize log(D(G(z))) instead of minimizing log(1 - D(G(z)))?
Ans: In early epochs, when the Generator produces poor samples, the Discriminator rejects them with high confidence (D(G(z)) ≈ 0). The derivative of log(1 - D(G(z))) is nearly flat near zero, causing severe vanishing gradients. Maximizing log(D(G(z))) provides strong, non-vanishing gradient signals early in training.
Q5. Why is Tanh used as the final activation layer in the Generator?
Ans: Tanh bounds the output strictly to [-1, 1], which provides zero-centered symmetric outputs. When paired with input images normalized to [-1, 1], it yields faster convergence and prevents numerical saturation compared to asymmetric activations like Sigmoid [0, 1].
